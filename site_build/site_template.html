The project in plain English

The question. Can you estimate how long people in a country live, on average, from a handful of health and economic statistics, such as how many adults die young, how common HIV/AIDS is, and how much schooling people get?

The honest answer. For a country the model has never seen, its estimate is typically within about ${miss_group_1} years of the real figure. The first test suggested about ${miss_random_1} years, but that test let the model peek at other years of the same country, a bit like grading a student on questions they have already seen.

What we learned. Three statistics do most of the work: HIV/AIDS deaths, adult deaths, and an index of income and education. A fancier model, fine-tuning, or adjusting the answers afterwards does not help; the model is limited by what the data contains, not by the method. It struggles most with a few countries and is somewhat less accurate for developing countries. Every result was checked on countries the model never saw.

What would make it better. Better information, not a better algorithm: data on conflict, epidemics and how reliable each country's reporting is.

Key findings

  • The headline score was too good. A random train/test split gives R² ${r2_random}, because other years of the same country sit in the training data. Holding out whole countries drops it to ${r2_group}, and the average miss roughly doubles (${miss_random} to ${miss_group} years). The lower number is the one to trust.
  • Three inputs do most of the work. HIV/AIDS deaths, adult mortality and the income-composition index lead by permutation importance (the drop in accuracy when an input is scrambled), which agrees with the model's built-in ranking.
  • Tuning didn't help. A 36-setting grid search scored with country-held-out folds ended at R² ${tuned_r2}, essentially the same as the defaults (${default_r2}). Judged by random folds, the same search picks a different winner, another effect of same-country overlap.
  • The algorithm isn't the bottleneck. Gradient boosting, histogram boosting and linear regression score ${sd_gb}, ${sd_hgb} and ${sd_lin} on the same unseen countries. The three tree models are effectively tied, so the Random Forest stays.
  • The "pull toward the middle" can't be corrected away, because it isn't a bias. The lowest life expectancies are over-predicted by ${shr_low} years on average, but grouped by what the model predicted, the errors are close to zero (calibration slope ${shr_slope}, where 1 is perfect). That is regression to the mean, which any imperfect model shows. Two correction steps, tested on unseen countries, made things slightly worse (average miss ${shr_mae_raw} → ${shr_mae_lin} and ${shr_mae_iso} years).
  • A few countries are very hard. ${top_country} is missed by ${top_country_miss} years. The worst 10% of countries carry ${top10_share}% of all error, and every model pulls the extremes toward the middle: too high for the lowest life expectancies, too low for the highest.
  • The uncertainty band is slightly optimistic. An 80% interval covers ${cover_random}% of random-split rows but only ${cover_group}% on unseen countries.
  • Developing countries are missed by more, and it isn't just because they're harder. On unseen countries the model misses developed countries by ${fair_dev_mae} years and developing ones by ${fair_dvg_mae}. The gap (${fair_gap} years, likely between ${fair_ci_lo} and ${fair_ci_hi}) holds even when countries with the same life expectancy are compared, and the uncertainty band covers ${fair_cov_dev}% of developed rows but only ${fair_cov_dvg}% of developing ones. Neither group is over- or under-predicted on average. Status is not a model input; six advanced economies the source data labels "Developing" were relabelled first.
  • So the uncertainty band is now sized per group. Widening developing countries' band by about ${cal_factor_dvg}% brings it to ${cal_cov_dvg}% coverage, with developed countries at ${cal_cov_dev}%, both close to the 80% the band promises. The widening was tested on countries that played no part in choosing it.
  • A data leak was found and fixed, and it barely mattered. Outliers had been capped using all rows before the train/test split, and life expectancy itself was capped (${leak_rows} low values raised). Capping is now learned from the training rows only. Unseen-country R² went from ${leak_old_group} to ${leak_new_group}, so the findings hold. Separately, 606 health-spending figures recorded as 0 (every country in 2015) were really "not reported"; treating them as missing also changed nothing important.

How it was done

  1. Cleaned the WHO data: normalized headers, treated impossible zeros as missing, filled gaps by country over time, capped outliers (limits learned from training rows only) and dropped near-duplicate columns. The pipeline is driven by one config file.
  2. Trained a 200-tree Random Forest and scored it two ways: a random split and country-grouped cross-validation.
  3. Diagnosed it: residuals, permutation importance, partial dependence, interval calibration, tuning, a four-model showdown, a hardest-countries investigation, a leak check and a developed-vs-developing fairness check.
  4. Tested. 37 automated tests check the cleaning, the leak-free clipping, that no country is ever on both sides of the honest test, and that this page quotes the same numbers as the saved results. They run on every change.
  5. Published this page from the saved results with one script, so every number and chart matches the latest training run.

Limits. The data ends in 2015 and has about 16 years per country, so it describes the past, not today. Some countries have thin or filled-in data; a country with no data at all for an input gets the all-country median. The model finds associations, not causes, and shouldn't be used to make decisions about people.
