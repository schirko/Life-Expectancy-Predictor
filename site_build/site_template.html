Life Expectancy Predictor

A machine-learning model that estimates a country's life expectancy from health and economic indicators, built on ${n_rows} country-year records from ${n_countries} countries. The focus is honesty: how well does it do on a country it has never seen, and where does it fail?

Key findings

  • The headline score was too good. A random train/test split gives R² ${r2_random}, because other years of the same country sit in the training data. Holding out whole countries drops it to ${r2_group}, and the average miss roughly doubles (${miss_random} to ${miss_group} years). The lower number is the one to trust.
  • Three inputs do most of the work. HIV/AIDS deaths, adult mortality and the income-composition index lead by permutation importance (the drop in accuracy when an input is scrambled), which agrees with the model's built-in ranking.
  • Tuning didn't help. A 36-setting grid search scored with country-held-out folds ended at R² ${tuned_r2}, essentially the same as the defaults (${default_r2}). Judged by random folds, the same search picks a different winner, another effect of same-country overlap.
  • The algorithm isn't the bottleneck. Gradient boosting, histogram boosting and linear regression score ${sd_gb}, ${sd_hgb} and ${sd_lin} on the same unseen countries. The three tree models are effectively tied, so the Random Forest stays (it also supports SHAP explanations).
  • A few countries are very hard. ${top_country} is missed by ${top_country_miss} years. The worst 10% of countries carry ${top10_share}% of all error, and every model pulls the extremes toward the middle: too high for the lowest life expectancies, too low for the highest.
  • The uncertainty band is slightly optimistic. An 80% interval covers ${cover_random}% of random-split rows but only ${cover_group}% on unseen countries.
  • A data leak was found and fixed, and it barely mattered. Outliers had been capped using all rows before the train/test split, and life expectancy itself was capped (${leak_rows} low values raised). Capping is now learned from the training rows only. Unseen-country R² went from ${leak_old_group} to ${leak_new_group}, so the findings hold.

How it was done

  1. Cleaned the WHO data: normalized headers, treated impossible zeros as missing, filled gaps by country over time, capped outliers (limits learned from training rows only) and dropped near-duplicate columns. The pipeline is driven by one config file.
  2. Trained a 200-tree Random Forest and scored it two ways: a random split and country-grouped cross-validation.
  3. Diagnosed it: residuals, permutation importance, partial dependence, interval calibration, tuning, a four-model showdown and a hardest-countries investigation.
  4. Published this page from the saved results with one script, so every number and chart matches the latest training run.

Limits. The data ends in 2015 and has about 16 years per country, so it describes the past, not today. Some countries have thin or filled-in data; a country with no data at all for an input gets the all-country median. The model finds associations, not causes, and shouldn't be used to make decisions about people.
